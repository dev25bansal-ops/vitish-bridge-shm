2.2 What the Problem is Really Asking
In civil engineering terminology, Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) is the process of implementing a
damage detection and characterization strategy for engineering structures such as bridges, buildings, and
offshore platforms. The damage is defined as changes to the material and/or geometric properties of these
systems, including changes to the boundary conditions and system connectivity, which adversely affect the
system’s current or future performance. The classical SHM pipeline involves five stages: sensing, data
acquisition, feature extraction, statistical modeling, and decision-making. A modern AI-driven SHM system
automates stages 3–5 using machine learning, replacing traditional threshold-based alarms with learned
anomaly detectors.
The problem statement is therefore asking for a closed-loop system that continuously monitors the structure
(rather than relying on periodic visual inspection), uses AI to detect early-stage damage indicators (rather than
waiting for visible failure), and presents the results through an interactive digital twin (rather than a static PDF
inspection report). The Morbi collapse — caused by corroded cables, broken anchor pins, and loose bolts that
were not detected during the last inspection — is the canonical example of failure under the legacy
manual-inspection regime.

3. Global Bridge Failure Context
3.1 India — The Operational Hotspot
India is the world’s most acute bridge-failure hotspot in absolute terms. A Newslaundry analysis published in
July 2025 documented 170 bridge collapses across India between 2021 and 2025, claiming 202 lives and
injuring 441 more. The National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) Annual Report on Accidental Deaths and
Suicides in India (ADSI) provides the official government dataset via Dataful. The most devastating single
incident was the Morbi suspension bridge collapse on 30 October 2022, in which 135 people died when a
century-old colonial-era pedestrian bridge over the Machchhu River in Gujarat gave way under the weight of
an estimated 400 visitors. The Special Investigation Team (SIT) final report attributed the failure to multiple
maintenance lapses: corroded cables, broken anchor pins, and loose bolts that had not been flagged during the
Oreva Group’s recently-completed renovation contract.
Other notable Indian bridge failures in recent years include the Majerhat bridge collapse in Kolkata
(September 2018, 3 dead), the Andaman minibridge collapse (October 2022), the Sikkim floods that destroyed
14 bridges (October 2023), and the Pune-Goa highway bridge incident (2024). The Central Road Research
Institute (CRRI) maintains an active list of distressed bridge investigation projects, including the Kolaghat
Bridge in Shahjahanpur, Uttar Pradesh. IDDC India reports conducting inventory and condition surveys
covering 13,496.4 metres of National Highway bridges in Uttar Pradesh and Uttarakhand alone, indicating the
scale of the inspection backlog.

4. Solution Architecture
The proposed architecture is a four-layer stack designed to satisfy all four expected-solution components (IoT
sensors, computer vision, digital twins, predictive maintenance) while remaining buildable within a 36-hour
hackathon window. Each layer is independently testable — if any single layer fails during the demo, the
remaining three can still show value, providing graceful degradation.

4.1 Layer 1 — Edge / Sensing
The sensing layer is the physical interface between the bridge and the digital system. It comprises three
categories of devices. First, MEMS accelerometers (Analog Devices ADXL355, recommended for its
ultra-low noise density of 25 µg/√Hz) measure three-axis vibration at 100 Hz sampling rate, mounted at piers,
mid-span, and abutments. Second, cameras (Raspberry Pi Camera v2 for fixed CCTV positions; DJI Tello or
similar consumer drone for periodic aerial inspection) capture high-resolution imagery of bridge decks, piers,

The choice of ESP32 as the edge microcontroller is deliberate: at approximately USD 10 per unit, with built-in
Wi-Fi and Bluetooth Low Energy, deep sleep current draw of 10 µA, and a dual-core 240 MHz processor, it is
the cheapest viable platform for edge inference. The ESP32 can also run TensorFlow Lite Micro models
directly, allowing a lightweight vibration anomaly detector to run on-device — useful when bandwidth is
constrained or for store-and-forward operation during network outages. A single 18650 Li-ion cell with a small
solar panel provides 30+ days of autonomous operation, critical for bridges in remote areas without grid
power.
4.2 Layer 2 — Communication
The communication layer uses MQTT (Message Queuing Telemetry Transport) as the primary protocol, with
Mosquitto as the broker. MQTT was chosen over HTTP for three reasons: (1) its publish/subscribe model
maps naturally to many-sensor-to-one-dashboard topologies; (2) its lightweight header (2 bytes) reduces
bandwidth costs on 4G/LTE connections; (3) its three Quality of Service levels (0, 1, 2) allow per-topic
reliability configuration — critical vibration alerts use QoS 2 (exactly once), routine temperature telemetry
uses QoS 0 (fire and forget). LoRaWAN serves as a fallback for bridges outside cellular coverage, providing
10 km range at the cost of 50 bps throughput. All communication is encrypted via TLS 1.3 with mutual
certificate authentication to prevent sensor spoofing attacks.
4.3 Layer 3 — AI Processing
The AI layer runs two parallel inference pipelines that fuse their outputs into a unified Bridge Health Index.
The computer-vision branch uses YOLOv8-seg fine-tuned on the dacl10k bridge damage dataset (10,000+
images, 19 defect classes) and SDNET2018 (56,000 crack/no-crack images). The vibration branch uses an
LSTM Autoencoder trained on the Z24 Bridge benchmark dataset (the most-cited SHM vibration dataset, with
690+ citations, collected from a real 60-metre bridge in Switzerland before its demolition). A late-fusion
engine combines the two branches’ confidence scores with age and traffic factors to produce a single Bridge
Health Index in the range 0–100. Section 9 covers the AI methodology in detail.
4.4 Layer 4 — Digital Twin & Dashboard
The digital twin is the user-facing layer and the differentiator that separates this solution from sensor-only
competitors. Built with React Three Fiber (a React renderer for Three.js), it renders a 3D model of the
monitored bridge with sensor markers color-coded by status (green = normal, amber = warning, red = critical).

7. AI/ML Methodology
The AI layer runs two parallel inference branches that fuse their outputs into a unified Bridge Health Index
(BHI). The two-branch design reflects a fundamental reality of SHM: visual defects (cracks, spalling) and
vibration anomalies measure different failure modes at different time scales. Cracks develop over months and
are visible long before they affect structural stiffness. Vibration anomalies reflect stiffness loss in real time but
cannot identify the specific defect type. Fusing both modalities gives a more complete picture than either
alone.
 Figure 7.1 — Multi-modal ML pipeline. The vision branch (left) and vibration branch (right) are trained independently, then fused
 via a weighted late-fusion layer into the Bridge Health Index.
7.1 Branch A — Computer Vision (YOLOv8-seg)
The vision branch uses YOLOv8-seg with a CSPDarknet backbone, pre-trained on COCO and fine-tuned on a
combination of dacl10k (10,000 bridge-specific images, 19 defect classes) and SDNET2018 (56,000
crack/no-crack images). The training regimen uses a 70/20/10 train/val/test split, input resolution 640×640,
batch size 16, AdamW optimizer with cosine learning-rate decay from 1e-3 to 1e-5 over 50 epochs. The
expected mean Average Precision (mAP) at IoU 0.5 is 0.65+, with inference speed of approximately 30 FPS
on an NVIDIA Jetson Nano and 200+ FPS on a single A100. The output is a per-frame set of bounding boxes
with class labels (crack, spalling, corrosion, exposed rebar, efflorescence) and per-pixel segmentation masks.

7.2 Branch B — Vibration Anomaly Detection (LSTM Autoencoder)
The vibration branch uses a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) Autoencoder trained on the Z24 Bridge
benchmark dataset. The autoencoder takes a 1024-sample window of three-axis acceleration data (10.24
seconds at 100 Hz) and learns to reconstruct it. The hypothesis is that an autoencoder trained on healthy-bridge
data will produce high reconstruction error on damaged-bridge data, because the temporal patterns of damaged
vibration are outside the training distribution. The anomaly score is the mean squared reconstruction error per
window, normalized by the threshold = mean + 3σ on the validation set. The final score is sigmoid(error −
threshold), producing a vib_anomaly value in [0, 1]. Expected F1 score on the Z24 progressive-damage test set
is approximately 0.85+.
The reference implementation is the Elios-Lab WaveNet+MiniRocket repository on GitHub, which provides a
working Z24 SHM pipeline with state-of-the-art results. We will adapt this codebase rather than training from
scratch, saving 6–8 hours of development time. For validation, the trained model is evaluated on the
Vänersborg Bridge dataset (Sweden) to demonstrate cross-bridge generalization — a critical point for Q&A;
defense.
7.3 Late Fusion — Bridge Health Index
The two branches are combined via weighted late fusion. The Bridge Health Index (BHI) is defined as:
 BHI = 100 × (1 − 0.6 × CV_confidence − 0.4 × vib_anomaly) × age_factor × traffic_factor
where CV_confidence is the max class confidence from the vision branch (0 = no defect detected, 1 = severe
defect with high confidence), vib_anomaly is the sigmoid-normalized LSTM reconstruction error, age_factor
decays from 1.0 (new bridge) to 0.7 (50+ years old), and traffic_factor reflects average daily truck loading
(1.0 for low, 0.85 for high). The weights 0.6/0.4 reflect the higher reliability of direct visual evidence versus
indirect vibration inference. The BHI is recomputed every 60 seconds and displayed as a time-series on the
dashboard.

The digital twin is the layer that transforms this project from a sensor-monitoring system into something
judges will remember. A rotating 3D bridge with live sensor markers, clickable vibration spectra, and a health
index that visibly degrades over time is the visual artifact that separates a winning demo from a competent one.
The implementation uses React Three Fiber (R3F), a React renderer for Three.js, because it provides
declarative component composition, integrates cleanly with React state management for live data updates, and
benefits from the drei helper library (OrbitControls, Environment, Grid, Html overlays).
8.1 Why Three.js over Unity or WebGL-native
Three primary alternatives exist for browser-based 3D rendering: raw WebGL, Unity WebGL build, and
Three.js. Raw WebGL requires 300+ lines of boilerplate for a single textured mesh. Unity WebGL produces
20+ MB bundles and is overkill for a single-bridge visualization. Three.js hits the sweet spot: 600 KB minified
gzipped, declarative scene graph, mature camera/lighting/material APIs, and excellent documentation. React
Three Fiber wraps Three.js in React semantics — instead of writing imperative scene.add(mesh) calls, you
write components. This is the same ergonomic leap that React brought to DOM manipulation.
8.2 Bridge 3D Model Strategy
Two viable paths exist for sourcing the bridge model. Path A (recommended for hackathon time-pressure): use
a pre-built Sketchfab model. The "Bridge Truss Structure" model
(sketchfab.com/3d-models/bridge-truss-structure-3cecc079502a4092956188c79655f0d5) is CC-licensed,
downloadable as .glb, and loads directly via drei’s useGLTF hook. Path B (recommended for visual
differentiation): build a parametric bridge in Three.js using TubeGeometry for cables, ExtrudeGeometry for
the deck, and BoxGeometry for piers. Path B takes 4–6 hours but yields a model that matches the Morbi
bridge aesthetic (suspension cable-stay design) and can be animated to show the failure sequence. For the
demo, Path A as the safe baseline with Path B as a stretch goal is the right call.

8.4 Real-Time Data Overlay
Live data flows from the ESP32 edge sensors via MQTT to a Node.js backend, which translates MQTT

8.5 Performance Considerations
Three.js performance on consumer laptops is the single biggest risk to the demo. Three mitigations are
essential. First, use instancedMesh for sensor markers (one draw call for 50 sensors instead of 50 draw calls).
Second, set the Canvas dpr parameter to [1, 1.5] to cap pixel ratio — high-DPI displays otherwise render 4x
more pixels than necessary. Third, use React.memo on SensorMarker to prevent re-renders when sibling
markers update. With these optimizations, a scene with 50 sensors, a 50k-polygon bridge model, real-time
WebSocket data, and live charts runs at 60 FPS on a 2020 MacBook Air.
9. India Policy & Regulatory Landscape
The policy timing for an AI-based SHM platform in India has never been more favorable. Four converging

9.1 Indian Bridge Management System (IBMS)
The Indian Bridge Management System (IBMS), launched in October 2016 by then-Minister of Road
Transport and Highways Nitin Gadkari, was established to create a national inventory of all bridges on India’s
National Highways and to rate their structural condition. The IBMS database currently catalogues over
172,000 bridges with condition ratings on a 0–9 scale, generated through periodic visual inspections by PWD
engineers. In June 2026, MoRTH launched a nationwide digital survey covering 1.7 lakh bridges on National
Highways, using a new mobile-based application for field engineers to collect inventory and condition data on
smartphones and tablets. The platform generates annual inventory and ratings, and analyses the data to indicate
which bridges need repair or replacement.
The critical gap in IBMS is its reliance on annual visual inspection. Annual inspection means that a bridge can
develop a critical defect in month 2 of the cycle and remain undetected for 10 months. The Morbi collapse —
which occurred just months after a renovation inspection — is the canonical failure of annual visual regimes.
Our AI-driven SHM platform fills this gap by providing continuous monitoring (1-second updates instead of
annual inspections) and AI-augmented defect detection (catching hairline cracks and vibration anomalies a
human inspector would miss). The pitch to IBMS is complementary, not competitive: we are the real-time

16.1 Technology Evolution
Three technology trends will shape the platform’s evolution beyond the initial 36-month roadmap. Foundation
models for SHM (arxiv 2404.02944, April 2024) promise a single pretrained model transferable across
bridges without per-bridge fine-tuning — this would eliminate the Z24 training dependency and reduce
onboarding time for new bridges from weeks to hours. Federated learning across bridges would allow the
model to improve continuously as more bridges come online, without centrally pooling sensitive sensor data
(critical for government customers). Satellite-based change detection using Sentinel-2 and HLS imagery
would extend monitoring to bridges without installed sensors, providing coarse but universal coverage as a
"first alert" layer.
